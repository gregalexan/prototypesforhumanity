## District Court of The Hague, cases number 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19

Back to: : Search results
District Court of The Hague, cases number 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19
Netherlands
District Court of The Hague, cases number 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19
Rechtbank Den Haag, Zaaknummer 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19
Case-law
Criminal repression
Date: 17.11.2022
Summary
Relevant facts: Flight MH17 was a KLM flight on its way from Amsterdam to Kuala Lumpur when it was shot above eastern Ukraine on 17 July 2014. All 283 passengers and 15 crew members died.  
Key findings of the court: The court simultaneously rendered four judgments, one for each of the accused. The first three accused, a pro-Russian Ukrainian and two Russians, were convicted to life imprisonment for having caused the crash of flight MH17 and for having murdered 298 persons, while the fourth accused, of Russian nationality, was acquitted. The four judgements are identical as concerns the District Court’s reasoning (in the Dutch legal system, each defendant receives a separate judgment).
Relevance of the court's findings to IHL: In the part on admissibility, the District Court classified the situation in eastern Ukraine on 17 July 2014. After having established that an armed conflict was ongoing, and after having initially determined it was a non-international armed conflict based on the criteria of organisation and protracted violence (intensity) as defined by the ICTY case-law, the Court explored whether the conflict could be considered international on the basis of the relationship between the Russian Federation and the Donetsk People's Republic (DPR). The court based its analysis on the following elements: (i) the background of DPR members, (ii) the support provided to DPR, (iii) the coordination and instructions received and (iv) the direct participation of the Russian Federation. The Court concluded that there was an international armed conflict ongoing between Ukraine and the Russian Federation on 17 July 2014, based on the overall control of the Russian Federation over DPR. The Court then analysed whether the defendants enjoyed combatant immunity for their acts. Based on its reading of Art. 43 AP I, the Court determined that overall control was not sufficient to establish that the defendants were under responsible command by the Russian Federation. For that, the Russian Federation would have to accept that the defendants belonged to the armed forces and would have to take responsibility for their actions. The Court thus decided that the defendants, as members of the DPR, did not enjoy combatant status under IHL, and therefore did not enjoy combatant immunity. 
Procedural Status: This is a first instance decision. Key words: international armed conflict, overall control, combatant status, combatant immunity. Relative laws and case-law: The Court referred to Art. 43 API for the definition of combatant, when assessing whether the accused could claim the combatant immunity. The Court considered that it was not necessary for the above-mentioned assessment to be also made against the criteria of Art. 4(A) GCIII. In the District Court’s view, this article does not deal with combatants and their privileges and immunities, but with the separate question of the status of prisoners of war.
Dutch Criminal Code: Art. 168 (intentionally and unlawfully causing the aircraft to crash), Arts. 287 and 289 (murder with premeditation)

Date: 22.11.2021
Summary
The judgement related to New Zealand’s handling of visa applications for families of Afghan citizens who had worked as interpreters for the NZDF in Afghanistan or supported NZDF troops in other ways. Many former interpreters working with the NZDF are now residents and citizens of New Zealand. The application concerned extended family members in danger in the current circumstances in Afghanistan including because of this connection to the NZDF. The family members’ applications for New Zealand visas had been handled, but not completed, largely due to intervening COVID 19-related border restrictions in New Zealand. Although the issue was resolved in the applicant’s favour on other grounds, Justice Cooke accepted the general relevance of IHL and acknowledged that certain IHL obligations continue after the cessation of active hostilities. The decision found that the circumstances of the families connected with the NZDF’s previous military operations would be ‘too remote for there to be any continuing international obligation on New Zealand to effectively protect those remaining in Afghanistan’ [33]. Justice Cooke stated that ‘New Zealand might be thought to have a form of moral responsibility for the citizens of Afghanistan who assisted them in the years while they were there, but the suggestion that there is an international obligation to the wider family of those persons after hostilities has ceased stretches the argument on international humanitarian law obligations too far’ [33].

BGH, Urteil vom 28. Januar 2021 - 3 StR 564/19 - OLG München
Case-law
Criminal repression
Date: 28.01.2021
Summary
The defendant, a member of the Afghan military forces, appeared in two videos. In one occasion with another military official, he severely beat three detained fighters and threatened them during an interrogation. In another occasion, he paraded the body of a Taliban deceased fighter, while kicking and insulting the body among other acts, and subsequently hanged the body.
On 28 January 2021, the Federal Court of Justice confirmed the Higher Regional Court’s judgement of 26 July 2019 on the part where it stated that the defendant was not immune from war crime charges committed in 2013-2014 during the NIAC. It founded its opinion on a comparative analysis of different national and international legal precedent.
However, the Court quashed part of the judgement on the ground that the treatment of the prisoners is to be assessed as war crime against persons of cruel or inhuman treatment through torture within the meaning of Section 8(1)(3) VStGB (Code of Crimes against International Law) as interpreted in light of the Rome State and the third Geneva Convention of 1949, in addition to the charge of war crime against persons of gravely humiliating or degrading treatment under Section 8(1)(9) VStGB. It considered that the mistreatment of the first prisoner also had psychological effects on the two other prisoners, so that the acts should be considered as a single action triggering the threshold of Section 8(1)(3) VStGB which do not require grave bodily harm or (attempted) coercion. Therefore, the case was referred back to the Higher Regional Court for a new hearing and new sentence replacing the previous penalty (2 years' probation). The judgement is final.
Relevance to IHL: physical acts and psychological effects triggering the qualification of war crime against persons committed through torture.

## District Court of The Hague, cases number 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19

Back to: : Search results
District Court of The Hague, cases number 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19
Netherlands
District Court of The Hague, cases number 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19
Rechtbank Den Haag, Zaaknummer 09-748004/19, 09-748005/19, 09-748006/19, 09-748007/19
Case-law
Criminal repression
Date: 17.11.2022
Summary
Relevant facts: Flight MH17 was a KLM flight on its way from Amsterdam to Kuala Lumpur when it was shot above eastern Ukraine on 17 July 2014. All 283 passengers and 15 crew members died.  
Key findings of the court: The court simultaneously rendered four judgments, one for each of the accused. The first three accused, a pro-Russian Ukrainian and two Russians, were convicted to life imprisonment for having caused the crash of flight MH17 and for having murdered 298 persons, while the fourth accused, of Russian nationality, was acquitted. The four judgements are identical as concerns the District Court’s reasoning (in the Dutch legal system, each defendant receives a separate judgment).
Relevance of the court's findings to IHL: In the part on admissibility, the District Court classified the situation in eastern Ukraine on 17 July 2014. After having established that an armed conflict was ongoing, and after having initially determined it was a non-international armed conflict based on the criteria of organisation and protracted violence (intensity) as defined by the ICTY case-law, the Court explored whether the conflict could be considered international on the basis of the relationship between the Russian Federation and the Donetsk People's Republic (DPR). The court based its analysis on the following elements: (i) the background of DPR members, (ii) the support provided to DPR, (iii) the coordination and instructions received and (iv) the direct participation of the Russian Federation. The Court concluded that there was an international armed conflict ongoing between Ukraine and the Russian Federation on 17 July 2014, based on the overall control of the Russian Federation over DPR. The Court then analysed whether the defendants enjoyed combatant immunity for their acts. Based on its reading of Art. 43 AP I, the Court determined that overall control was not sufficient to establish that the defendants were under responsible command by the Russian Federation. For that, the Russian Federation would have to accept that the defendants belonged to the armed forces and would have to take responsibility for their actions. The Court thus decided that the defendants, as members of the DPR, did not enjoy combatant status under IHL, and therefore did not enjoy combatant immunity. 
Procedural Status: This is a first instance decision. Key words: international armed conflict, overall control, combatant status, combatant immunity. Relative laws and case-law: The Court referred to Art. 43 API for the definition of combatant, when assessing whether the accused could claim the combatant immunity. The Court considered that it was not necessary for the above-mentioned assessment to be also made against the criteria of Art. 4(A) GCIII. In the District Court’s view, this article does not deal with combatants and their privileges and immunities, but with the separate question of the status of prisoners of war.
Dutch Criminal Code: Art. 168 (intentionally and unlawfully causing the aircraft to crash), Arts. 287 and 289 (murder with premeditation)

Case-law
Criminal repression
Date: 14.04.2022
Summary
The defendant was the commander and the head of the Department of Political Affairs of the Pul-e-Charki prison in Kabul in the 1980s. The prison was reportedly notorious for its inhumane regime during the Soviet-Afghan war, with detainees being illegally imprisoned, tortured and subjected to inhumane and degrading treatment, as well as executed. 
On 12 November 2019, the Dutch police arrested the defendant man. He reportedly had arrived in the Netherlands in 2001 and later obtained Dutch nationality under a false name. Investigators of the International Crimes Team received dozens of witness statements testifying to the suspect’s involvement in the crimes. In 2020, the prosecutors have reportedly gathered witness statements from victims and former employees in the Afghan communist government, demonstrating that the suspect worked in three different positions at the Pul-e-Charki prison.
The defendant was accused of involvement in war crimes, so the court considered whether the requirements for the application of international humanitarian law had been met in this case, including whether there was an armed conflict during the indicted period, whether the defendant knew about it, whether the victims named in the indictment were protected persons and whether there was a close connection between the alleged conduct and the armed conflict. 
In its judgment, the District Court of The Hague sentenced the defendant to 12 years of imprisonment for war crimes. Together and in association with others, he committed serious violations of international humanitarian law such as arbitrary detention, cruel and inhuman treatment and outrage upon personal dignity of at least 18 persons in the period from 1983 to 1987 in the Pul-e-Charkhi prison in Afghanistan.

The judgement related to New Zealand’s handling of visa applications for families of Afghan citizens who had worked as interpreters for the NZDF in Afghanistan or supported NZDF troops in other ways. Many former interpreters working with the NZDF are now residents and citizens of New Zealand. The application concerned extended family members in danger in the current circumstances in Afghanistan including because of this connection to the NZDF. The family members’ applications for New Zealand visas had been handled, but not completed, largely due to intervening COVID 19-related border restrictions in New Zealand. As a result of this, the families in question had not been eligible for emergency evacuation carried out from Kabul Airport by the NZDF alongside its coalition partners in August 2021 – prompting their application. Although the issue was resolved in the applicant’s favour on other grounds, Justice Cooke accepted the general relevance of IHL and acknowledged that certain IHL obligations continue after the cessation of active hostilities. The decision found that the circumstances of the families connected with the NZDF’s previous military operations would be ‘too remote for there to be any continuing international obligation on New Zealand to effectively protect those remaining in Afghanistan’ [33]. Justice Cooke stated that ‘New Zealand might be thought to have a form of moral responsibility for the citizens of Afghanistan who assisted them in the years while they were there, but the suggestion that there is an international obligation to the wider family of those persons after hostilities has ceased stretches the argument on international humanitarian law obligations too far’ [33].

## Federal Court of Justice of Münich, Case 3 StR 564/19

Back to: : Search results
Federal Court of Justice of Münich, Case 3 StR 564/19
Germany
BGH, Judgment of 28 January 2021 - 3 StR 564/19 - OLG Munich
BGH, Urteil vom 28. Januar 2021 - 3 StR 564/19 - OLG München
Case-law
Criminal repression
Date: 28.01.2021
Summary
The defendant, a member of the Afghan military forces, appeared in two videos. In one occasion with another military official, he severely beat three detained fighters and threatened them during an interrogation. In another occasion, he paraded the body of a Taliban deceased fighter, while kicking and insulting the body among other acts, and subsequently hanged the body.
On 28 January 2021, the Federal Court of Justice confirmed the Higher Regional Court’s judgement of 26 July 2019 on the part where it stated that the defendant was not immune from war crime charges committed in 2013-2014 during the NIAC. It founded its opinion on a comparative analysis of different national and international legal precedent.
However, the Court quashed part of the judgement on the ground that the treatment of the prisoners is to be assessed as war crime against persons of cruel or inhuman treatment through torture within the meaning of Section 8(1)(3) VStGB (Code of Crimes against International Law) as interpreted in light of the Rome State and the third Geneva Convention of 1949, in addition to the charge of war crime against persons of gravely humiliating or degrading treatment under Section 8(1)(9) VStGB. It considered that the mistreatment of the first prisoner also had psychological effects on the two other prisoners, so that the acts should be considered as a single action triggering the threshold of Section 8(1)(3) VStGB which do not require grave bodily harm or (attempted) coercion. Therefore, the case was referred back to the Higher Regional Court for a new hearing and new sentence replacing the previous penalty (2 years' probation). The judgement is final.
Relevance to IHL: physical acts and psychological effects triggering the qualification of war crime against persons committed through torture.
Related law: Section 8(1), point (3) and (9), and Section 1(1) VStGB.
Related case law: BGH, judgment of 27 July 2017, case 3 StR 57/17.
